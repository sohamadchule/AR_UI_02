# Akruti — Competitor Business Research

**Research date:** 2026-10-27
**Scope:** furniture retailers that run AR/3D, and the vendors that sell AR/3D to retailers.

### Rules applied throughout

- Funding raised is **not** revenue. Valuation is **not** revenue.
- A parent's revenue is **not** a subsidiary's.
- **Inter IKEA Group and Ingka Group figures are never added together** — they are
  separate companies in a franchisor/franchisee relationship.
- Every figure carries its financial-year period.
- Figures that could not be verified are labelled **"not publicly verified."** They are
  not estimated and not filled in.

### Evidence classification

| Tag | Meaning |
| --- | ------- |
| `[FILING]` | Company's own annual report, earnings release or regulatory filing. |
| `[AGGREGATOR]` | Third-party database of registry filings (Tofler etc.). Verify before relying on. |
| `[VENDOR]` | Company's own marketing. Methodology usually undisclosed. |
| `[REVIEW]` | Customer review platform. Note incentive bias. |
| `[PRESS]` | Journalism or press release. |
| `[INFERENCE]` | The author's reasoning, flagged as such. |
| `[UNVERIFIED]` | Looked for, not found. |

**Read this carefully.** Wayfair is the most AR-invested furniture retailer in the world
and in FY2025 it: grew 5.1%, turned $17M of operating income on $12.5bn of revenue, and
still lost $313M. **Active customers declined.** It spent $1.43bn on advertising —
**115× its operating income**.

`[INFERENCE]` Two conclusions for Akruti, and they point in opposite directions:
- The *bullish* read: if the category leader with the best AR still can't make the model
  work, AR alone is not a business — it has to attach to something a retailer already
  pays for.
- The *sober* read: Wayfair has spent a decade and enormous sums on 3D/AR and **does not
  claim it as a revenue driver anywhere in its reporting.** If it were the growth lever
  vendors say it is, this is the one company that would say so.

**Technology:** in-house. Wayfair built its own 3D asset pipeline and photorealistic
rendering org, and publishes assets openly — the **CC BY 4.0 chair currently sitting in
Akruti's own catalogue** is a Wayfair release (see `ar-product-model-audit.md` §3).

### 2.4 Houzz

## 3. AR/3D vendors — what they sell and what it costs

### 3.1 3D Cloud (formerly Marxent)

Enterprise 3D platform: Web AR, room visualisers, product configurators, 3D room planners.
Named enterprise customers: **Lowe's, Ashley, Macy's** `[VENDOR]`.
Verticals: furniture, kitchen, bath, decking/outdoor, storage, closets.

**Pricing:** *"Contact vendor for pricing"*
([Capterra](https://www.capterra.com/p/246051/3D-Cloud/)). **Not public.**
Vendor claims *"Launch in just 8-10 weeks."*

**Documented customer friction** `[REVIEW]`, from the Capterra listing:
- *"The web room planner isn't mobile-friendly"*
- *"Change management can be difficult and expensive"*
- *"Not the cheapest option"*
- *"Some models take a few weeks to produce"*

> **Bias warning, and it is severe:** nearly all 10 reviews are rated 5.0 and are marked
> vendor-referred with an incentive offered. Treat the praise as marketing. **Treat the
> criticism as significant** — these are the complaints that survived an incentivised
> review process.

`[INFERENCE]` An 8–10 week launch, enterprise logos, opaque pricing and "not the cheapest"
together describe a company selling six-figure annual contracts to national retail chains.
It is not competing for an independent shop in Pune, and it does not want to.

### 3.2 Cylindo

Furniture-specific 3D visualisation: Cylindo Viewer, Cylindo AR, Cylindo Studio.
**Pricing:** *"Pricing available upon request"*
([Software Advice](https://www.softwareadvice.com/product-configurator/cylindo-profile/)).

**Documented friction** `[REVIEW]` (only 2 reviews, both 5/5, one incentivised):
- *"Rendering timelines can be a bit slow with large projects"*
- Adding new materials is **expensive**, because re-rendering large collections compounds
- **"Smaller, cheaper items like accessories don't make financial sense to ingest"**

`[INFERENCE]` That last point is the most revealing sentence in this document about vendor
economics. A paying enterprise customer is saying the **per-SKU cost of getting an item
into the system exceeds the item's value**. That is a structural limit on every per-model
pricing model — and it is precisely the wall an Indian retailer with 400 low-ASP SKUs
would hit on day one.

### 3.3 GlamAR (Fynd, India)

The most transparent competitor on *structure*, though not on price
([pricing](https://www.glamar.io/pricing),
[3D model creation](https://www.glamar.io/pricing/3d-model-creation)).

| Dimension | What GlamAR publishes |
| --------- | --------------------- |
| Model | *"a one-time setup fee along with a monthly recurring cost, determined by the number of try-ons per month"* |
| Tiers | Starter / Growth / Scale at **100 / 300 / 500 SKUs** |
| Volume discount | 5% / 10% / 15% |
| Onboarding | **2–4 weeks**, depending on modules and whether 3D assets already exist |
| Scope | *"Each subscription is valid for one project, encompassing one iOS, one Android, one HTML5, and one macOS application"* |
| Scale claim | 1,000 to 1M+ SKUs; *"No limits on the number of visitors, user sessions, interactions, or clicks"* |
| Prices | **Not public** — quote on request |
| Trial | Free SDK trial |

`[INFERENCE]` Two things Akruti should take from this. First, **setup fee + usage-based
monthly is the shape of the market** — worth matching, because buyers will already
understand it. Second, SKU tiers starting at 100 tell you the floor: GlamAR's smallest
plan already assumes a 100-SKU 3D catalogue. An Indian shop with 30 digitised products is
below their entry point. **That gap is Akruti's opening.**

## 4. The low-cost and free alternatives — Akruti's real competition

`[INFERENCE]` Akruti will lose far more deals to "we'll just use the free thing" or
"we'll do nothing" than to 3D Cloud. These deserve more attention than the enterprise
vendors.

| Alternative | Cost to a retailer | What it gives | What it does **not** solve |
| ----------- | ------------------ | ------------- | -------------------------- |
| **Google `<model-viewer>`** | **Free**, Apache-2.0 | A production-grade 3D viewer + AR on Android and iOS, in ~10 lines of HTML | Nothing upstream: no 3D models, no dimension verification, no catalogue management, no analytics, no fit checking. **Akruti itself is built on it.** |
| **Shopify native 3D/AR** | Included in the platform (**₹1,994/mo** Basic in India) | Model hosting via the 3D Warehouse app, AR Quick Look, a marketplace of 3D modelling partners `[VENDOR]` | Model *creation* is still bought separately from partners. Only helps merchants already on Shopify. |
| **Apple Quick Look / Reality Composer** | Free | Excellent iOS AR from a USDZ | iOS only; no catalogue, no dimensions |
| **Three.js / WebXR direct** | Free | Total control | You build everything — which is what Akruti has been doing |
| **Freelance 3D on Fiverr/Upwork** | Per model | Models | No platform, no QA, no consistency, no pipeline |
| **Doing nothing** | ₹0 | — | The actual default. **This is the competitor to beat.** |

> **The hardest strategic fact in this document:** the viewer layer — the part Akruti has
> built — is *free and commoditised*. `<model-viewer>` is Apache-2.0 and Google maintains
> it. Shopify bundles AR at ₹1,994/month. **Nobody will pay for a 3D viewer.**
>
> What is scarce, expensive and unsolved is everything *around* it: getting a real
> retailer's real products into correctly-dimensioned 3D, cheaply, repeatably, and
> proving it was worth it. That is where a defensible business can exist — and it is the
> part of Akruti that is currently entirely unbuilt (`ar-product-model-audit.md` §3).

**What Akruti should ask on a competitor sales call,** since none of them publish:

1. Total first-year cost for a 50-SKU catalogue, all-in.
2. Setup/onboarding fee separately from recurring.
3. Cost per additional model, and cost to change a material or colourway on an existing
   model *(Cylindo's reviewers flagged this as the expensive one)*.
4. Minimum contract term and minimum annual value.
5. Who produces the 3D models, who owns them, and can they be exported if the contract ends.
6. Time from signature to first live product page.
7. Is there an India price list, or is it USD-denominated?

Item 6 is the discriminator. If the honest answer is 8–10 weeks, Akruti's answer needs to
be days.

## 6. Claimed business impact — and why to distrust it

The two statistics quoted most often in this industry:

**"AR/3D content shows a 94% higher conversion rate."**
Origin: **Shopify's own 2020 social post** `[VENDOR]` —
*"Interactions with products having 3D/AR content showed a 94% higher conversion rate than
for products without AR/3D"*
([source](https://x.com/Shopify/status/1306973590814949376)).

Three problems, each fatal on its own:
1. It is **correlational**, not causal. It compares products that *have* 3D with products
   that do not.
2. **Selection bias on the product.** Merchants add 3D to their hero SKUs — higher
   consideration, better photography, more marketing. Those convert better anyway.
3. **Selection bias on the user.** It counts *interactions*. A shopper who chose to rotate
   a 3D model was already further down the funnel than one who scrolled past.

No methodology, sample size, or control group has ever been published.

**"Up to 250% conversion improvement."**
Shopify's 2018 AR launch post `[VENDOR]`, citing Rebecca Minkoff and Bumbleride
([source](https://www.shopify.com/blog/shopify-ar)). No baseline, no sample, no method.

### What *is* reasonably well evidenced

Furniture returns, from NRF/Coresight/Optoro data compiled by
[Eightx](https://eightx.co/blog/average-furniture-and-home-return-rate-benchmarks):

| Figure | Value | Source |
| ------ | ----- | ------ |
| Furniture, **online** return rate | ~**19–23%** (midpoint 22.7%) | NRF + Coresight, compiled |
| All categories, online | 19.3% | NRF + Happy Returns, *2025 Retail Returns Landscape* |
| Apparel, online | 25% | compiled |
| Furniture, **cross-channel** | ~8% | Statista-cited |
| **Top furniture return reason: size/space mismatch** | **~58%** | 2025 vendor category analysis |
| Colour/material gap | ~44% | YouGov 2025 (broad retail, not furniture-specific) |
| Transit damage | ~31% | ClaimLane 2026 |
| **Cost per furniture return** | **$55–108** (midpoint $72–80); Optoro: 27% of purchase price | Optoro, Pitney Bowes BOXpoll, BLS |
| Cost per apparel return | ~$30 | compiled |

**The source's own caveat, which must travel with the numbers:** these figures come from
different survey populations and **do not share a denominator**, so they establish *rank
order*, not shares of a single pool.

`[INFERENCE]` **This is the honest version of Akruti's business case**, and it is
genuinely stronger than the vendor hype:

> Roughly a fifth of online furniture orders come back. The single largest stated reason
> is that the item did not fit the space. Each return costs the retailer $55–108 — two to
> three times an apparel return, because furniture is heavy and damages easily.

That argument does not require anyone to believe a 94% conversion lift. It requires only
that a retailer agrees size-related returns are expensive — which they already know,
because they pay for the trucks.

**But** — the caveat that must be stated to any Indian retailer — **these are US figures,
on US online furniture, with free-returns norms.** Indian furniture retail is largely
offline, heavily cash-on-delivery, and has different return practice. **No equivalent
Indian furniture return-rate data was located in this research.** That is unresolved
question #1 below.

## 7. Unresolved research questions

Ranked by how much the answer would change Akruti's strategy.

1. **What is the actual return/cancellation rate for furniture in India, and what does a
   return cost an Indian retailer in rupees?** All return economics above are US. Without
   an Indian figure, the headline ROI argument cannot be made to an Indian buyer.
   *How to resolve:* ask 10 retailers directly for their return/cancellation rate and
   their average reverse-logistics cost.
2. **Does an offline Indian showroom have a problem AR solves at all?** The customer is
   standing in front of the sofa. The real question is *"will it fit in my living room"* —
   which is a **customer's-home** problem, not a showroom problem. This inverts the
   product: the AR may need to run in the *customer's* home, launched from a QR code the
   salesperson hands them. **Akruti's existing QR architecture is already well suited to
   this** — see `ar-market-opportunity.md` §5.
3. **What do Indian retailers currently pay for any software at all?** Shopify Basic at
   ₹1,994/mo is the anchor, but how many SMB furniture retailers pay even that?
4. **What is the true delivered cost of one usable, correctly-dimensioned 3D model in
   India**, including QA and dimension verification? US rates are $40–260. Akruti's
   margin depends entirely on this number and it has never been measured in this project.
5. **Revenue, headcount and churn for 3D Cloud, Avataar, GlamAR, Cylindo, Threedium,
   Emersya, Zolak.** All private, none verified. UK Companies House (Threedium), French
   registries (Emersya) and Indian MCA (Avataar, GlamAR/Fynd) are the places to look next.
6. **Is there any independent, peer-reviewed study of AR's effect on furniture conversion
   or returns?** Everything located was vendor-sourced or correlational.
7. **What does Shopify's native AR actually cost a merchant end-to-end**, including
   partner-supplied models? This sets the price ceiling.
8. **Houzz Pro's pricing and retention.** The closest analogue to a workflow-attached
   visualisation business, and the least studied.

## 8. What the evidence actually supports

Stated carefully, because the temptation in this category is to overclaim.

**Supported:**
- Furniture online return rates (~19–23%) exceed the all-category average, size mismatch
  is the leading stated cause, and furniture returns cost 2–3× apparel returns. `[FILING/compiled]`
- Enterprise 3D vendors are genuinely expensive, genuinely slow to implement (8–10 weeks,
  2–4 weeks), and explicitly uneconomic for low-value SKUs. `[REVIEW/VENDOR]`
- No vendor serves a retailer with fewer than ~100 SKUs. `[VENDOR]`
- The viewer layer is free and commoditised. `[OFFICIAL]`
- Indian online furniture is small and shrinking at the top. `[AGGREGATOR]`
- The pure-play 3D-content business has not scaled in public markets. `[FILING]`

**Not supported:**
- That AR causes a 94% or 250% conversion lift. Both trace to vendor posts with no method.
- That AR demonstrably reduces furniture returns. Plausible, widely asserted, **not
  independently demonstrated** in anything located here.
- That Indian retailers are currently willing to pay for AR. **No evidence either way was
  found. This is the assumption the entire business rests on, and it is untested.**
